# European Accessibility Act: code review checklist

Source: Directive (EU) 2019/882 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 17 April 2019 on the accessibility requirements for products and services (the European Accessibility Act), https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj. The accessibility requirements are in Annex I. Its technical route is the harmonised standard EN 301 549 V3.2.1 (2021-03), "Accessibility requirements for ICT products and services", https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/301500_301599/301549/03.02.01_60/en_301549v030201p.pdf, which the Commission cited in Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/1339. Version 4.1.1 of EN 301 549 was published in September 2026 and moves the web baseline to WCAG 2.2. We haven't confirmed that it has been cited in the Official Journal, so these checks use the V3.2.1 clause numbers, which stay valid in V4.1.1 for the web and software criteria checked here, as far as we know. Check the clause numbers against the version your project works to. The checks below are written in our own words. "Annex I" numbers are the Directive's section and point numbers, and the other numbers are EN 301 549 clause numbers, so a finding can be traced back to the official text.

The Directive applies from 28 June 2025 to the products and services it lists. For software, these are mainly consumer banking services, e-commerce services, electronic communications services, e-books and their reading software, passenger transport websites and apps, audiovisual media service access, and self-service terminals. Micro-enterprises that provide services are exempt from most obligations (Article 4(5)). Member States may add national rules.

This checklist covers the criteria that changed code can show. Others, such as captions, audio description and the quality of text alternatives, depend on content that a code review can't see. Contrast, target size, reflow and focus visibility depend on the rendered page. Flag them only when the values are visible in the code and the problem is clear. Otherwise give the standard `needs_check` and say what a person should test.

## When this applies

Apply these checks to changed code that renders or styles a user interface for a covered service or product, in a project that offers it to consumers in the EU. This includes HTML, JSX, TSX and template markup, CSS, shared UI component libraries, native UI code (SwiftUI, UIKit, Android XML layouts, Jetpack Compose, React Native, Flutter), and front-end logic that handles focus, keyboard input, dialogs, form validation or status messages.

If the change has no user interface code, such as a backend, database, infrastructure or test-only change, or the service isn't covered by the Directive, report this standard as `not_applicable`. A change to UI text alone still needs the label and error message checks.

## Checks

### [EAA Annex I III(c)] Websites, online applications and mobile apps meet the perceivable, operable, understandable and robust requirement
Look for: new or changed screens that break any of the more specific checks below. Use this ID for web or app interface problems that fit none of them, such as a required step that only works with a mouse or a touch gesture.
Decided from code: partly. This is the Directive's general requirement. Whether an issue is a breach is judged against EN 301 549, and the clause checks below are the code-visible parts.

### [EAA Annex I III(b)] Information about the service and its accessibility is available in accessible form
Look for: help pages, accessibility statements, terms, order confirmations, e-tickets, invoices and e-books generated by the changed code as images or untagged PDFs only, with no text version. Instructions that depend on one sense, such as "click the green button". Service information with no text alternative for non-text content.
Decided from code: partly. The content of the information is outside the code, but its format and structure are visible.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.1.1.1] Images and icons have a text alternative, and decorative ones are hidden
Look for: `<img>` with no `alt`, or with `alt` that is a file name or just "image". Icon-only buttons and links with no accessible name from text, `aria-label` or `aria-labelledby`. Meaningful inline `<svg>` with no title or label. This maps to WCAG success criterion 1.1.1.
Not a breach: decorative images marked so that assistive technology skips them, such as `alt=""` or `aria-hidden="true"`.
Decided from code: yes. Whether the text describes the image well may need a person.

### [EAA EN 301 549 11.1.1.1.1] Native app images and icons have a text alternative
Look for: iOS images and controls with no `accessibilityLabel`. Android `ImageView`s, `ImageButton`s and Compose `Image`s with no content description. React Native and Flutter touchables with no accessible label. This is the software counterpart of clause 9.1.1.1, for apps that aren't web pages.
Not a breach: decorative images marked as such, such as `contentDescription = null` in Compose or `importantForAccessibility="no"`.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.1.3.1] Structure that is shown visually is also in the markup
Look for: headings made with bold or large text instead of `<h1>` to `<h6>`. Lists built from `<div>`s. Data tables with no `<th>` cells. Form fields with no programmatic label. Related radio buttons or checkboxes with no `<fieldset>` and `<legend>`. Lost landmark elements such as `<main>` and `<nav>`. This maps to WCAG success criterion 1.3.1.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.1.4.3] Text has enough contrast with its background
Look for: a text colour and its background colour both set in the changed code, as literal values or as design tokens defined in this repository, with a contrast ratio below 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text). Light grey placeholder text and lowered opacity on text are the usual problems. This maps to WCAG success criterion 1.4.3.
Not a breach: text in disabled controls, pure decoration, and logos.
Decided from code: partly. Flag only when both colours are visible in the code. Text over images or gradients and themes chosen at run time need the rendered page.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.2.1.1] Everything can be done with a keyboard
Look for: click handlers on `<div>`s or `<span>`s with no `tabindex`, role and key handlers. `tabindex` values above 0 that scramble the order. Hover-only menus. Custom controls that handle only mouse or touch events. Drag-only interactions with no keyboard route. This maps to WCAG success criterion 2.1.1.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.2.4.7] Keyboard focus is always visible
Look for: `outline: none` or `outline: 0` on focusable elements with no replacement `:focus` or `:focus-visible` style. Focus rings removed in a global reset. Custom components that never show focus. This maps to WCAG success criterion 2.4.7.
Decided from code: yes, for styles in the changed code.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.3.3.1] Form errors are pointed out and described in text
Look for: errors shown only by a red border or colour change. Error messages that aren't tied to the field, such as by `aria-describedby`, `aria-invalid` or an announced message. Generic "invalid input" messages that don't say which field is wrong. This maps to WCAG success criterion 3.3.1.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.4.1.2] Custom controls expose their name, role and state
Look for: custom buttons, toggles, tabs, menus, dialogs, sliders and comboboxes built from generic elements with no role, accessible name or state (`aria-expanded`, `aria-checked`, `aria-selected`). Wrong or conflicting ARIA, such as `role="button"` on a link that navigates. State changes that update only the look. This maps to WCAG success criterion 4.1.2.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 9.4.1.3] Status messages are announced without moving focus
Look for: toasts, "added to basket" confirmations, search result counts, loading and error banners that appear with no `role="status"`, `role="alert"` or `aria-live` region. Live regions added to the page at the same moment as their content. This maps to WCAG success criterion 4.1.3.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 11.5.2.5] Native app controls expose their name, role and state to assistive technology
Look for: custom native views or canvas-drawn controls with no accessibility element, trait or role, or no name or value exposed. SwiftUI or UIKit custom controls with no `accessibilityTraits`. Android custom views with no `AccessibilityNodeInfo` or Compose `semantics`. Controls whose state change isn't reported.
Decided from code: yes.

### [EAA EN 301 549 11.7] The app respects the user's own accessibility settings
Look for: fixed font sizes that ignore Dynamic Type or the Android font scale. Hard-coded colours that override a high-contrast, dark or inverted mode. Animation that ignores a reduce-motion setting. Code that overrides the platform's screen reader or keyboard settings.
Decided from code: partly. Whether the layout copes with a larger text size needs a test on a device.

### [EAA EN 301 549 7.3] Players give users controls for captions and audio description
Look for: a custom media player with no caption or audio description control, or with such controls placed deeper in a menu than the volume control. Players that strip the caption track from a source that supplies one.
Decided from code: partly. Whether captions exist depends on content outside the code.

### [EAA EN 301 549 12.1.2] Product documentation and support information are accessible
Look for: help, onboarding, user guides and support pages that the changed code ships as images, scans or untagged PDFs. Support contact paths that need a phone call or a visual CAPTCHA with no alternative.
Decided from code: partly. The code shows the format, not the full content.
